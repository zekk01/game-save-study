### Newly examined sources and screening decisions

- James Bornholt, Antoine Kaufmann, Jialin Li, Arvind Krishnamurthy, Emina Torlak, and Xi Wang, “Specifying and Checking File System Crash-Consistency Models,” ASPLOS 2016, DOI 10.1145/2872362.2872406. The full paper explicitly analyzes the temporary-file/write/rename replacement pattern and documents that, under ext4 system crashes, rename can persist ahead of data writes, producing empty or partial files. It therefore directly cautions against describing temp-file replacement as generally power-loss safe. This source was already cited in the draft; the targeted review found its particularly close pattern-level analysis and the draft was revised to state it plainly. Primary author paper: https://syslab.cs.washington.edu/papers/ferrite-asplos16.pdf
- Juha Jeon, “Crash-Consistent Checkpointing for AI Training on macOS/APFS,” arXiv:2511.18323 (2025). The arXiv record describes an empirical comparison of checkpoint write modes, process-crash injection, SHA-256 integrity checking/rollback, and performance on one macOS/APFS setup. It is not a game-save study and uses different procedures and durability questions, but its experimental structure is close enough to materially narrow any novelty claim. It is a preprint; no peer-reviewed version was located during this pass. Primary record and full text: https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18323
- Hu et al., “TxFS: Leveraging File-System Crash Consistency to Provide ACID Transactions,” USENIX ATC 2018. Used as contextual prior work rather than a new numbered manuscript citation: it describes temporary-copy plus rename as a common application update method and discusses its performance/recovery costs relative to transactions. Official paper: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/atc18/atc18-hu.pdf

### Effect on the paper's contribution and fit

- The exact temp-file-then-rename procedure is not novel; formal crash-consistency literature studies it and shows why process termination and system crash/power-loss claims must remain separate.
- A recent non-game preprint already combines checkpoint update modes, process-crash injection, checksums, and performance measurements. The present study is differentiated only by its game-like state framing, Windows/.NET implementations, checkpoint classifications, and its specific chosen procedures. Those differences are modest and may not establish sufficient significance for a JEPCS Short Communication.
- Recommendation: do not claim a novel save technique or a first comparison. Before submission, first perform the broader database and citation-chaining review. If it finds more work on application-process termination, game persistence, or checkpoint comparisons, consider reframing as a replication/teaching case study or adding a stronger game-specific research question and evaluation. The current results are not yet strong evidence of broad originality.
- Search-screening exclusions from this pass: practitioner forum discussions and engine how-to pages were not treated as scholarly evidence; storage-system transaction papers were retained as context only when they explained update patterns; the AI-checkpoint preprint was included in the novelty-risk analysis but its unreviewed claims are not used as a foundation for reliability conclusions.

## What the search supports

- Application storage recovery depends on operation ordering and platform/file-system behavior.
- A paper using process termination must not imply machine-crash or durable-media behavior.
- Current game-save guidance describes temporary-file replacement and recommends local double buffering. Neither update pattern can be claimed as novel.
- The system literature contains many related crash-consistency, temporary-file/rename, logging, and transactional-storage approaches. These are not a direct match for the study implementation or process-stop model.
- Whittaker’s 2009 game-development thesis already compares XML/binary save/load performance and file size. Viotti and Kinderkhedia’s 2022 preprint broadly benchmarks JSON-compatible binary formats. This narrows the current study: it does not claim novelty for game-save benchmarking or format trade-offs; its distinct question is interrupted-update recovery behavior and costs.
- Peer-reviewed game-related persistence work exists for server-side relational persistence of MMOG/ROIA world and character state (Knemeyer et al.). It does not test the local save-file procedures in this study, but the paper must avoid implying that game data persistence itself is an unstudied topic.
- No exact match was located in this targeted search for the particular three-procedure game-like workload comparison. This supports a limited case-study framing only; it does not prove novelty and no priority claim should be made.

## Full-text screening of MMOG persistence leads (2026-11-03)

The OpenAlex lead was followed to full text for Kaiwen Zhang, *Persistent Transaction Models for Massively Multiplayer Online Games* (M.S. thesis, McGill University, 2010). The abstract describes consistency/transaction models and a persistence architecture integrated with the Mammoth research framework, with performance evaluation of action implementations and persistence support. Chapter 4 explicitly addresses durability and server fault tolerance; the architecture monitors state replicas and records updates to stable storage, then reconstructs state after restart. The thesis is important game-specific persistence and recovery context and narrows any broad novelty claim. Its subject is distributed/server-side MMOG state and transactional action persistence; it does not evaluate local save-file replacement, two-slot files, or application-process termination during a file update. Full text: https://www.collectionscanada.gc.ca/obj/thesescanada/vol2/002/MR72985.PDF.

A related peer-reviewed work is Kaiwen Zhang and Bettina Kemme, “Transaction Models for Massively Multiplayer Online Games,” in *Proceedings of the 30th IEEE International Symposium on Reliable Distributed Systems (SRDS)*, 2011, pp. 31–40, DOI 10.1109/SRDS.2011.13. Its abstract defines game-specific transaction consistency levels, evaluates their costs, and discusses durability/fault tolerance. This is online distributed transaction persistence, not local single-player save-file mechanics. Publisher-linked institutional record: https://pure.etsmtl.ca/en/publications/transaction-models-for-massively-multiplayer-online-games/.

Also screened the SIGMOD 2009 tutorial “Database Research in Computer Games,” which surveys game data management, online game persistence, and database engineering context. It is useful background but not an empirical local-save recovery study. Full text: https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=fae238a00c4dcb220ff29dd0b700c4bde5de0efe&repid=rep1&type=pdf.
